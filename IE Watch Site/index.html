Foreign-trade zones · Lease clauses

Thirty days to put your building in a foreign-trade zone. Your lease is what stops you.

The weekly-entry saving alone runs about $0.90 per square foot a year on a 150,000 square foot building, close to what five months of free rent is worth over a five-year term. The federal application turns on one sentence, and it is a sentence your lease almost certainly does not contain.

Landed cost has been repriced three times in fourteen months. The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on February 20. The 10% Section 122 stopgap expired July 24 and was replaced the same day by Section 301 forced-labor tariffs covering 60 economies, roughly 10% for the EU, Taiwan, Mexico, Canada and India, 12.5% for Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Thailand and Brazil, and about 37.5% all-in for China. Section 232 metals sit at 50%. The Penn Wharton Budget Model puts the average effective rate across all US imports at 6.7% as of July, nearly triple the 2.3% of January 2025, with $107 billion in IEEPA refunds certified through August. A tenant signing a five-year Inland Empire lease this quarter is committing to a building for a tariff regime nobody can describe yet.

A foreign-trade zone is the standing hedge against that, and it attaches to the building rather than to the company. Goods admitted to a zone sit outside the customs territory of the United States until they enter US commerce. Duty is deferred until withdrawal, never accrues at all on anything re-exported or destroyed, and the importer files one weekly customs entry in place of one entry per shipment. Under the Alternative Site Framework, a usage-driven site inside an approved service area is approved by the Foreign-Trade Zones Board within thirty days on a short application. Riverside County holds its own grant, FTZ 244. San Bernardino County is served through FTZ 50, the Long Beach grantee, which operates Subzone 4 in San Bernardino. Across most of this market the zone is not the constraint.

The constraint is in the paperwork. 15 CFR 400.21(d)(3)(i) requires the application to include a statement as to site ownership (if not owned by the applicant or proposed operator, evidence as to their legal right to use the site). For a tenant, that evidence is the lease. Activation with Customs then runs under 19 CFR 146.6, which requires a blueprint showing the buildings and every opening, a written procedures manual for the inventory control and recordkeeping system, the written concurrence of the grantee, and a Form 301 operator’s bond, after which the port director weighs the security, suitability, and fitness of the facility. Every one of those items describes the landlord’s building.

Now read your own lease against that list. The standard Inland Empire form permits warehousing and distribution of the tenant’s products, which is not the same thing as operating a customs zone on the landlord’s land, and a narrow use clause is thin evidence of a legal right to use the site for zone purposes. The fencing, segregation, access control and camera coverage that satisfy a port director are alterations, and they need a consent the landlord is free to withhold, to price, or simply never to answer. Nothing in a standard lease obliges a landlord to sign a consent, produce a site plan, or return a grantee’s call. A tenant three years into a seven-year term has no leverage to make any of it happen, and the landlord has no reason to help for free at the exact moment the tenant has disclosed that the zone is worth money.

In zone →  52 entries × $651.50 =  $33,878
Saving = $135,512/yr = $0.90/SF/yr on 150,000 SF

The weekly-entry saving is the part that does not depend on what you import. The merchandise processing fee is 0.3464% of entered value, with a $651.50 cap per entry for the fiscal year ending September 30, so any entry above roughly $188,000 in value pays the cap. An importer filing one entry each business day pays that cap 260 times a year. Inside a zone it files 52 times.

Set that against what this market is actually paying for concessions. Across 260 Inland Empire industrial lease comps in our own file with an off-market date in 2025 or 2026, the median free rent concession is five months and the median deal is 138,442 square feet. Five months free on a sixty-month term at the $1.03 market asking rent is worth $1.03 per square foot a year. The weekly-entry saving is 88% of that. It costs none of the negotiating capital that free rent costs, it is invisible to the landlord’s rent comparison, and it does not stop in month six.

Then read the surrender clause, because the zone build-out is the part nobody prices. The tenant pays for the fencing, the segregation and the inventory control system. At expiration a standard restoration clause either makes the tenant strip it out at its own cost, or leaves it in place, at which point the landlord owns a building that is materially easier to lease to the next importer. Neither outcome was negotiated. Both were settled by language written before anyone at that table was thinking about customs.

What to ask for, and when

Ask at the lease event, where it costs nothing. One sentence does most of the work: Landlord shall reasonably cooperate, at no cost to Landlord, with Tenant’s application for foreign-trade zone designation and activation of the Premises, including executing such consents, site plans and grantee acknowledgements as the Foreign-Trade Zones Board or U.S. Customs and Border Protection reasonably requires. Pair it with a use clause broad enough to name zone operations, an alterations carve-out for the security and segregation work Customs requires, and a surrender provision that states plainly whether the improvements stay and who pays if they go. Before you ask for any of it, call the grantee and confirm the building sits inside an approved service area. That call takes five minutes and decides whether the clause is worth anything at all. If you are already mid-term, raise it at the next amendment, expansion or extension, because those are the moments a landlord wants something from you.

The pull-forward ahead of the July 24 Section 301 duties was supposed to borrow from August. It mostly did not. Regulatory and tax load on IE industrial tenants

In force · phasing · watch

AB 98 / SB 415 warehouse standards

State · Warehouse Concentration Region
Phasing in
Statewide siting, buffer, loading-bay and truck-route rules for logistics development. Access requirements began Jan 1, 2026; the truck-route deadline slipped to 2030 for smaller jurisdictions, with 2028 for the rest. San Bernardino County jurisdictions have largely adopted routes; Riverside County has made little progress. Enhanced standards near sensitive receptors trigger on zone changes or buildings over 250,000 SF. For tenants: constrains new modern supply near labor and rooftops, and a reconfiguration of an existing building can pull a project into the standards.

South Coast AQMD Rule 2305 (WAIRE)

Regional · Warehouses over 100,000 SF
In force
All three size phases are in annual reporting. Points are earned through zero- and near-emission equipment, chargers, or clean truck visits, or bought out at $1,000 per point plus 6.25% admin. For tenants: a recurring occupancy cost that belongs in the lease. Issue 01 costs it out at $0.40/SF for a 300,000 SF operator.

Tariff regime after the IEEPA ruling

Federal · Importers and 3PLs
In force
The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on Feb 20, 2026, leaving $166–179B in duties in refund litigation. The 10% Section 122 stopgap expired July 24 and was replaced the same day by Section 301 forced-labor tariffs on 60 economies — roughly 10% for the EU, Taiwan, Mexico, Canada and India; 12.5% for Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Thailand and Brazil; about 37.5% all-in for China. Section 232 metals stay at 50%. For tenants: landed-cost volatility drives inventory strategy, which drives square footage. Importers sitting on refund claims may have balance-sheet capacity that does not show in current credit.

Cap-and-invest tightening

State · Energy and fuel costs
In force
The tightened cap took effect September 1. CARB pulled 118 million allowances from the 2027–2030 budgets, roughly an 11% cut to the cap through the end of the decade and about 7% a year to 2045, alongside a $4B manufacturing decarbonization fund. The August 2026 auction settled at $32.48 for current vintage allowances, up 12.7% from $28.81 in May. CARB’s own modeling of the amended program projects a weighted-average allowance price near $68 against a $44 baseline. California, Québec and Washington signed a linkage agreement on June 25 for joint auctions, expected to start between late 2027 and early 2028. For tenants: allowance prices feed electricity and diesel costs, and a NNN utility clause that does not distinguish a regulatory surcharge from a rate increase passes the whole move through. A doubling from here is the regulator’s own central case, not a bear case. Check the clause before modeling a 10-year occupancy pro forma.

Local moratoria and mitigation fees

Municipal · Entitlement risk
Watch
Redlands is furthest along. Its Planning Commission recommended Ordinance Text Amendment No. 372 on May 12 by 5–1, prohibiting new warehouses over 50,000 SF in every zoning district; the City Council voted unanimously on September 1 to delay final adoption to October 6 while staff rewrite the provisions covering existing buildings. Under the draft, an existing warehouse may add truck docks, convert to refrigerated space, or raise its height by up to 15%, but only by conditional use permit. Beaumont, Moreno Valley and Fontana have each rejected recent industrial proposals, and Moreno Valley is negotiating an air-quality abatement fee with the Attorney General’s Office. For tenants: in a city that adopts this, an expansion option in your lease stops being a right and becomes a discretionary approval, with environmental review and traffic studies attached. If your growth plan depends on adding docks or cube to the building you are in, confirm the city will still let you before you commit to the term.

Macro backdrop

Q2 2026 actuals and current bank forecasts
  • The Fed raised rates on September 16, moving the target range to 3.75%–4.00% on a unanimous 12–0 vote. The statement says inflation “remains elevated” and frames the increase as support for “a timelier return” to the 2% goal.
  • Wells Fargo called it. It moved in August to forecast a hike before year-end, citing tariffs, elevated oil and AI data-center power demand. Bank of America had core PCE easing to 2.8% by Q4, a path that implied cuts. September settled the argument in Wells Fargo’s favor.
  • GDP grew 1.5% in Q2, below the 1.8% expected and down from 2.1% in Q1. Consumer spending carried it at a 3.2% annual clip; business investment ex-housing rose 8.4% on AI buildout.
  • Inflation is why. Headline PCE +3.7% year over year, core +3.3%, both well above target, with energy disruption from the Iran conflict feeding through.
  • Labor is tighter than the growth rate suggests. Private payrolls averaged 88,000 a month in the first half, roughly three times last year’s pace; initial claims hit 187,000 in July, the lowest since 1969. Locally the picture is softer: Inland Empire unemployment is 5.5% and the labor force shrank 1.0% over twelve months.
  • CoStar’s September 24 read on the Inland Empire puts vacancy at 8.7% for 2026 Q3 and availability at 12.5%, a 15-year high, against a 6.2% historical average vacancy. Trailing 12-month net absorption is 6.0M SF versus a prior 10-year average of 17.5M SF, deliveries 7.7M SF, and market asking rent growth −0.9%. Construction underway is 14.0M SF, down from the 45.7M SF peak in 2022, with less than 10M SF forecast for completion across 2026 and 2027.
What a hike does to the renewal question

Relocation is capital. Racking, conveyor, dock equipment, moving the operation, the months of duplicate rent: most of it gets financed, and financing just got more expensive rather than less. That strengthens the case for negotiating hard where you already sit and weakens the cheap-capital case for moving. For owners it cuts the other way on a 2027 refinance, which is the same pressure that makes a landlord willing to trade on rent to keep a covenant in place.

Why it matters here

Loaded imports, not total TEUs, are what convert into pallet positions in Fontana and Perris. Empties are roughly 37% of the combined count, and repositioning them tells you nothing about occupancy demand.
