This week · Submarket spotlight
Moreno Valley · Perris · AB 98 truck routes

Moreno Valley is redrawing your trucks' route to the freeway. Your lease leaves you the bill.

Moreno Valley's proposed truck route map would take through trucks off Perris Boulevard, Alessandro, Heacock, Frederick, Cactus and four other segments, and the city's own consultant says that would add 8,296 truck miles a day. On a 300,000 SF building on Perris Boulevard shipping a quarter of its loads east, that would be about $294,000 a year. A route change takes no land and counts as a new law, so the standard lease leaves every mile with you.

On a 300,000 SF distribution building fronting Perris Boulevard near San Michele Road in south Moreno Valley, the city's proposed truck route map would add about $294,000 a year in truck miles, $0.98/SF/yr, if a quarter of your round trips head east on SR-60. That is 7.9% of IE East asking rent. Nothing in a standard Inland Empire lease moves any of it to the landlord.

AB 98 turned the truck route into a moving target. Government Code 65302.02(a) requires every city and county in the Warehouse Concentration Region to “[i]dentify and establish specific travel routes for the transport of goods, materials, or freight” that “[m]aximize the use of interstate or state divided highways.” Collector streets qualify only “when strictly necessary to reach existing industrial zones,” and trucks “shall be routed via transportation arteries that minimize exposure to sensitive receptors.” The deadline was January 1, 2026. Eight of the 14 jurisdictions missed it, Moreno Valley, Perris and Riverside County among them, and the Attorney General sent them an enforcement advisory on October 5. The penalties, up to $50,000 every six months, fall on the cities. The maps are being redrawn now, in the middle of your term.

Moreno Valley's proposal, PEN26-0058, strips through-truck status from segments of nine streets, including Perris Blvd, Alessandro Blvd, Heacock St, Frederick St and Cactus Ave, and concentrates trucks on SR-60 and I-215. The city's traffic consultant, Kimley-Horn, puts the citywide result at 8,296 more truck miles a day at 2024 volumes, up 2.8%. At ATRI's 2025 operating cost of $2.336 a mile, that is about $5.8 million a year. Perris Blvd carried up to 1,671 trucks a day in 2024. The consultant's reroute for it: “divert truck traffic to San Michelle Avenue, Heacock Street, Cactus Avenue, I-215, and then SR 60.” By 2040 the model has Perris Blvd truck miles falling from 2,274 a day to 113.

Your dock stays reachable. Vehicle Code 35703 bars a city from blocking commercial vehicles “coming from an unrestricted street” by direct route to deliver or pick up, and the draft ordinance sends trucks by “the shortest practical route” between a designated route and their origin or destination. What you lose is the short haul to the freeway. Direction decides how much. From Perris Blvd at San Michele, a westbound trip toward I-215, LA and the ports runs about the same distance on the new network. An eastbound SR-60 trip that ran about 4.5 miles straight up Perris Blvd now runs west and north to the 60/215 junction and back east, about 10.1 miles, 5.6 miles longer each way. Those distances are our grid estimate, not a measured routing, and if the city lets your trucks treat the old stretch of Perris Blvd as their direct route to SR-60, the eastbound cost shrinks.

Added cost/yr = eastbound round trips/day × added miles per round trip × $2.336/mile × operating days
Westbound ≈ 0 added miles; eastbound ≈ +11.2 miles per round trip (Perris Blvd at San Michele)

You pay either way. A private fleet pays by the mile; a carrier reprices the lane at the next rate negotiation. The cost shows up in drayage rates, never as a lease line, so nobody negotiated it.

The AIR CRE Standard Industrial/Commercial Single-Tenant Lease touches this in three places, and all three leave it with you. In ¶2.4 you acknowledge you satisfied yourself about the premises' “suitability for Lessee's intended use.” ¶14 gives relief only when the premises are “taken under the power of eminent domain.” And ¶6.3 makes you comply with Applicable Requirements, a defined term that covers ordinances, whenever they arrive:

“Except as otherwise provided in this Lease, Lessee shall, at Lessee's sole expense, fully, diligently and in a timely manner, materially comply with all Applicable Requirements … without regard to whether said Applicable Requirements are now in effect or become effective after the Start Date.”
The route change takes no land, so the condemnation clause never fires.

The route change takes no land, so the condemnation clause never fires. The building stays legal for its Agreed Use, so ¶6.1 offers no exit. ¶6.3 makes route compliance your job at your cost, and ¶2.4 stops you from arguing later that the building was sold to you as served by a particular route.

Moreno Valley adds a second exposure for anyone moving in or subleasing. Its draft logistics facilities ordinance (PEN26-0055) requires a Truck Routing Plan covering routing to designated truck routes, signage, pavement markings and a queuing analysis, revised “prior to a business license being issued by the city for any new tenant of the logistics use facility.” If your rent starts at substantial completion or delivery, it can start before the city lets you run trucks. Your subtenant faces the same gate.

None of it is final yet. The Moreno Valley council hearing on PEN26-0058 was set for September 1, 2026, both ordinances are still drafts with blank adoption dates, and the Attorney General listed the city as noncompliant on October 5. The truck route ordinance takes effect 30 days after second reading. Perris plans its council vote in December on an update that drops two stretches of Ellis Ave and one of San Jacinto Ave.

What to do about it, in order

1. Now, before Moreno Valley's second reading and Perris's December vote, pull the proposed maps (Moreno Valley's interactive map at moval.org/truckroutes; Perris's Initial Study, SCH 2025110111) and run your actual lanes against them. Split daily trips by direction. Eastbound SR-60 and cross-town moves are where the miles go. Comment letters go to transportation@moval.org; a tenant with 150 trucks a day carries weight at a hearing.

2. 15–24 months before expiration, put the route delta into the renewal rent argument. Option rent is set against “comparable” buildings, and a building that lost its direct truck route is no longer comparable to one on SR-60 frontage. Bring the city's own Kimley-Horn VMT memo to the table.

3. At renewal or in any new lease, add a truck route change clause. Sample language: “Truck Route Change. If, after the Effective Date, any governmental authority removes, restricts or re-designates any public truck route serving the Premises such that heavy-duty trucks cannot travel between the Premises and the nearest state highway or interstate interchange by the route available on the Effective Date, and the shortest lawful truck route between the Premises and that interchange increases by more than [one] mile, then (a) Base Rent shall be reduced by [$0.0X] per rentable square foot per month for the remainder of the Term, or (b) at Lessee's election, delivered within 180 days after the change takes effect, Lessee may terminate this Lease on [nine] months' written notice without penalty. Such a change shall not be deemed a Condemnation, an Applicable Requirement for purposes of Paragraph 6.3, or a matter assumed by Lessee under Paragraph 2.4.”

4. On a relocation into a Moreno Valley building, key rent commencement to the city's approval of your Truck Routing Plan and your business license, and make the landlord deliver the plan's signage and pavement markings at its cost. Sample language: “The Commencement Date shall not occur until the later of (i) Substantial Completion and (ii) the date the City of Moreno Valley approves Lessee's Truck Routing and Operations Plan and issues Lessee's business license, provided Lessee has submitted a complete application within [10] business days after Lessor's request. Lessor shall, at its sole cost, install all on-site signage and pavement markings required by the approved plan.”

5. Before you sublease or assign, remember your subtenant needs a revised Truck Routing Plan before it can get a business license. Write into the consent request, or into the lease now, that the landlord signs the plan application within 10 business days, and that the subtenant's operating date, not the sublease signing, starts any recapture or profit-sharing clock.

6. In CAM review, treat on-site truck route signage and markings as a landlord capital cost. The city's FAQ puts private property signage on the property owner. If your lease allows a pass-through, cap it as amortized capital.

What the Moreno Valley and Perris corridor actually signs

Lee Ontario’s own comps for the twelve months to October carry 34 closed industrial leases in Moreno Valley and Perris: median $0.944 NNN, median 172,000 SF, five months free and a median 65-month term. Across the whole Inland Empire the median closed lease is 21,100 SF at $1.160 on a 39-month term. The corridor leases big boxes long. A 65-month term signed this month runs into 2032, past every AB 98 truck-route deadline and past whatever Moreno Valley and Perris adopt in the next six months.

Regulatory and tax load on IE industrial tenants

In force · phasing · watch

AB 98 / SB 415 warehouse standards

State · Warehouse Concentration Region
Phasing in
Statewide siting, buffer, loading-bay and truck-route rules for logistics development. Access requirements began Jan 1, 2026, and so did the deadline for Warehouse Concentration Region cities to adopt truck routes. Eight of the 14 jurisdictions missed it, Moreno Valley, Perris and Riverside County among them, and the Attorney General sent them an enforcement advisory on October 5, 2026. Penalties run up to $50,000 every six months and fall on the cities, so the maps are being redrawn now, mid-term. Enhanced standards near sensitive receptors trigger on zone changes or buildings over 250,000 SF. For tenants: it constrains new modern supply near labor and rooftops, an addition to a building you already occupy can pull the whole development into the standards, and a route re-designation lengthens your haul to the freeway at your cost. Issue 06 runs the 20% test on an expansion option. Issue 07 costs Moreno Valley’s proposed map at about $294,000 a year on a 300,000 SF building.

South Coast AQMD Rule 2305 (WAIRE)

Regional · Warehouses over 100,000 SF
In force
All three size phases are in annual reporting. Points are earned through zero- and near-emission equipment, chargers, or clean truck visits, or bought out at $1,000 per point plus 6.25% admin. For tenants: a recurring occupancy cost that belongs in the lease. Issue 01 costs it out at $0.40/SF for a 300,000 SF operator.

Tariff regime after the IEEPA ruling

Federal · Importers and 3PLs
In force
The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on Feb 20, 2026, leaving $166–179B in duties in refund litigation. The 10% Section 122 stopgap expired July 24 and was replaced the same day by Section 301 forced-labor tariffs on 60 economies — roughly 10% for the EU, Taiwan, Mexico, Canada and India; 12.5% for Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Thailand and Brazil; about 37.5% all-in for China. Section 232 metals stay at 50%. For tenants: landed-cost volatility drives inventory strategy, which drives square footage. Importers sitting on refund claims may have balance-sheet capacity that does not show in current credit.

Cap-and-invest tightening

State · Energy and fuel costs
In force
The tightened cap took effect September 1. CARB pulled 118 million allowances from the 2027–2030 budgets, roughly an 11% cut to the cap through the end of the decade and about 7% a year to 2045, alongside a $4B manufacturing decarbonization fund. The August 2026 auction settled at $32.48 for current vintage allowances, up 12.7% from $28.81 in May. CARB’s own modeling of the amended program projects a weighted-average allowance price near $68 against a $44 baseline. California, Québec and Washington signed a linkage agreement on June 25 for joint auctions, expected to start between late 2027 and early 2028. For tenants: allowance prices feed electricity and diesel costs, and a NNN utility clause that does not distinguish a regulatory surcharge from a rate increase passes the whole move through. A doubling from here is the regulator’s own central case, not a bear case. Check the clause before modeling a 10-year occupancy pro forma.

Local moratoria and mitigation fees

Municipal · Entitlement risk
Watch
Redlands is furthest along. Its Planning Commission recommended Ordinance Text Amendment No. 372 on May 12 by 5–1, and on October 6 the City Council introduced Ordinance No. 3002 on a 5–0 vote, prohibiting new warehouses of 50,000 SF or more, or with more than five truck docks, in every zoning district. The council struck the provision that would have let existing warehouses add truck docks. An existing building may still request height up to its zoning maximum, generally 50 feet, by conditional use permit and with an energy audit showing an efficiency gain; the earlier 15% height cap and the economic-feasibility study requirement are gone. The ordinance returns for final adoption at a later meeting, with no date set. Beaumont, Moreno Valley and Fontana have each rejected recent industrial proposals, and Moreno Valley is negotiating an air-quality abatement fee with the Attorney General’s Office. Moreno Valley also has two drafts in play: PEN26-0058, which strips through-truck status from segments of nine streets, and PEN26-0055, which would require an approved Truck Routing Plan before the city issues a business license to any new tenant of a logistics facility. For tenants: in Redlands the dock-addition path is now closed outright rather than discretionary, so an expansion that depends on new docks in that city fails on the current draft regardless of what the lease says. Elsewhere, an expansion option stops being a right and becomes a discretionary approval with environmental review attached. If your growth plan depends on adding docks or cube to the building you are in, confirm the city will still permit it before you commit to the term.

Macro backdrop

Q2 2026 actuals and current bank forecasts
  • The Fed raised rates on September 16, moving the target range to 3.75%–4.00% on a unanimous 12–0 vote. The statement says inflation “remains elevated” and frames the increase as support for “a timelier return” to the 2% goal.
  • Wells Fargo called it. It moved in August to forecast a hike before year-end, citing tariffs, elevated oil and AI data-center power demand. Bank of America had core PCE easing to 2.8% by Q4, a path that implied cuts. September settled the argument in Wells Fargo’s favor, and the day after the hike Wells Fargo put the funds rate at 4.00%–4.25% by year-end with no changes through 2027, on the reasoning that the excess inflation is supply-driven and the labor market is not overheating.
  • GDP grew 1.5% in Q2, below the 1.8% expected and down from 2.1% in Q1. Consumer spending carried it at a 3.2% annual clip; business investment ex-housing rose 8.4% on AI buildout.
  • Inflation is why. Headline PCE +3.7% year over year, core +3.3%, both well above target, with energy disruption from the Iran conflict feeding through.
  • Labor is tighter than the growth rate suggests. Private payrolls averaged 88,000 a month in the first half, roughly three times last year’s pace; initial claims hit 187,000 in July, the lowest since 1969. Locally the picture is softer: Inland Empire unemployment is 5.5% and the labor force shrank 1.0% over twelve months.
  • CoStar’s September 24 read on the Inland Empire puts vacancy at 8.7% for 2026 Q3 and availability at 12.5%, a 15-year high, against a 6.2% historical average vacancy. Trailing 12-month net absorption is 6.0M SF versus a prior 10-year average of 17.5M SF, deliveries 7.7M SF, and market asking rent growth −0.9%. Construction underway is 14.0M SF, down from the 45.7M SF peak in 2022, with less than 10M SF forecast for completion across 2026 and 2027.
What a hike does to the renewal question

Relocation is capital. Racking, conveyor, dock equipment, moving the operation, the months of duplicate rent: most of it gets financed, and financing just got more expensive rather than less. That strengthens the case for negotiating hard where you already sit and weakens the cheap-capital case for moving. For owners it cuts the other way on a 2027 refinance, which is the same pressure that makes a landlord willing to trade on rent to keep a covenant in place.

Why it matters here

Loaded imports, not total TEUs, are what convert into pallet positions in Fontana and Perris. Empties are roughly 37% of the combined count, and repositioning them tells you nothing about occupancy demand.
