This week · Regulatory deep-dive
Measure C · Business license tax · E-commerce status

Perris voters approved a warehouse tax. Over 50,000 SF, your customer mix decides if you owe.

Perris voters approved a business license tax of up to $0.10/SF on non-e-commerce warehouse, industrial and manufacturing operators over 50,000 SF with large truck activity: $30,000 a year on 300,000 SF at the full rate. The city says operators pay it, and on that description it never shows on your NNN reconciliation and a tight tax paragraph does nothing about it. Whether you owe it turns on what your building ships and to whom, and a customer shift, a subtenant or an assignee can change that answer.

On a 300,000 SF non-e-commerce distribution building in Perris, Measure C costs $30,000 a year once billing starts, at the full $0.10/SF/yr, and $159,274 over five years if the rate rises at the 3% cap every year. That is $0.53/SF over the term, and 0.91% of Lee's Q3 2026 IE East average NNN of $0.915/SF/mo in year one. It will not decide a move by itself. It passed with 79.50% of the vote on the semi-final count, and the size matters less than who gets the bill and what can change it.

The AIR single-tenant net lease does not allocate this tax. The city describes it as “paid exclusively by qualifying warehouse and industrial operators” and “not a property tax,” and in a single-tenant NNN building the operator on that description is you. On that reading the bill comes straight to the tenant, outside the Real Property Taxes paragraph you negotiated, and nothing in the lease says whose e-commerce status controls.

September is the biggest month either port has had in two years

Combined volume hit 2,039,829 TEUs in September, 21.4% above September 2025 and past the 1,964,069 peak of July 2025. Long Beach carried it: 997,177 TEUs against 797,537 a year earlier, loaded inbound up 31.3% to 509,390. Los Angeles added 1,042,652 with 545,696 loaded imports. Calendar year to date the two ports are at 15,756,881 TEUs, 3.6% ahead of 2025, so the September jump is one strong month against a flat year rather than a step up in demand.

October reversed West and steadied East

West’s reported YTD absorption fell from 3.94M SF to 2.18M, which puts October itself at about negative 1.75M SF, and vacancy rose with it, 4.82% to 5.28%, while availability edged up to 12.16%. East went the other way: the YTD deficit narrowed from 209,172 SF to 20,513, an October of roughly positive 189,000, and availability dropped to 13.10%, though vacancy ticked up to 6.56%. Gross activity kept building on both sides, 47.4M SF across 523 transactions in West against 42.3M across 320 in East, so the average East deal is about 132,000 SF to West’s 91,000. Construction diverged: West rose to 6.38M SF across 16 projects while East fell to 3.04M across 11. Q3 asking is unchanged, West $1.034 and East $0.915, a 13% premium. The October absorption figures are the difference between two consecutive Lee YTD prints rather than a separately published monthly number.

The renewal discount is gone: new deals clear five cents lower with two more months free

Lee Ontario’s comp export, pulled November 1 and covering January 1 through October 31, carries 649 closed Inland Empire industrial leases: 498 new, 140 renewals, 11 expansions. At 100,000 SF and above on an NNN rate, 75 new leases clear at a median $0.95 on a 66-month term with six months free; 53 renewals clear at $1.00 on 64 months with four months free. Both still carry a 3.5% median escalation. Run the medians out and the renewal costs about 8.6% more in effective rent, $0.94/SF/mo against $0.86, because it pays five cents more on the face and gives up two months of abatement over a term two months shorter. On 300,000 SF that spread is roughly $266,000 a year. These are medians across different buildings and submarkets rather than a matched pair, so treat the gap as a reason to price the alternative, not as a quote. Unearned abatement is also the first thing a successor owner can decline to honor.

Regulatory and tax load on IE industrial tenants

In force · phasing · watch

AB 98 / SB 415 warehouse standards

State · Warehouse Concentration Region
Phasing in
Statewide siting, buffer, loading-bay and truck-route rules for logistics development. Access requirements began Jan 1, 2026, and so did the deadline for Warehouse Concentration Region cities to adopt truck routes. Eight of the 14 jurisdictions missed it, Moreno Valley, Perris and Riverside County among them, and the Attorney General sent them an enforcement advisory on October 5, 2026. Penalties run up to $50,000 every six months and fall on the cities, so the maps are being redrawn now, mid-term. Enhanced standards near sensitive receptors trigger on zone changes or buildings over 250,000 SF. For tenants: it constrains new modern supply near labor and rooftops, an addition to a building you already occupy can pull the whole development into the standards, and a route re-designation lengthens your haul to the freeway at your cost. Issue 06 runs the 20% test on an expansion option. Issue 07 costs Moreno Valley’s proposed map at about $294,000 a year on a 300,000 SF building.

South Coast AQMD Rule 2305 (WAIRE)

Regional · Warehouses over 100,000 SF
In force
All three size phases are in annual reporting. Points are earned through zero- and near-emission equipment, chargers, or clean truck visits, or bought out at $1,000 per point plus 6.25% admin. For tenants: a recurring occupancy cost that belongs in the lease. Issue 01 costs it out at $0.40/SF for a 300,000 SF operator.

Tariff regime after the IEEPA ruling

Federal · Importers and 3PLs
In force
The Supreme Court struck down the IEEPA tariffs on Feb 20, 2026, leaving $166–179B in duties in refund litigation. The 10% Section 122 stopgap expired July 24 and was replaced the same day by Section 301 forced-labor tariffs on 60 economies — roughly 10% for the EU, Taiwan, Mexico, Canada and India; 12.5% for Japan, Korea, Vietnam, Thailand and Brazil; about 37.5% all-in for China. Section 232 metals stay at 50%. The nearest date is November 9. USTR proposed on October 23, published October 26 at 91 Fed. Reg. 67388, to extend the suspension of its Section 301 maritime measures — the per-vessel fees on Chinese-owned, Chinese-operated and Chinese-built ships, the duties on Chinese-origin ship-to-shore cranes and cargo-handling equipment, and the Annex III fees on foreign-built vehicle carriers — from November 10 through January 9, 2027 (docket USTR-2026-0694). Comments closed October 30. Checked again on November 6: no final notice had been published that we could find, and the current suspension lapses at 11:59 p.m. Eastern on November 9, so the fees and crane duties resume November 10 unless one appears first. Separately, the October 28 Section 232 proclamation on robotics and industrial machinery found a threatened impairment but imposed no duty, directing Commerce and USTR to negotiate agreements and report within 180 days. For tenants: landed-cost volatility drives inventory strategy, which drives square footage. A lapse on November 10 lands on San Pedro Bay first and reaches your landed cost through the carriers. The robotics decision leaves imported automation and materials-handling equipment un-tariffed for now, so an automation capex case does not need a duty contingency this quarter, but 180 days is the horizon on it. Importers sitting on refund claims may have balance-sheet capacity that does not show in current credit.

Cap-and-invest tightening

State · Energy and fuel costs
In force
The tightened cap took effect September 1. CARB pulled 118 million allowances from the 2027–2030 budgets, roughly an 11% cut to the cap through the end of the decade and about 7% a year to 2045, alongside a $4B manufacturing decarbonization fund. The August 2026 auction settled at $32.48 for current vintage allowances, up 12.7% from $28.81 in May. CARB’s own modeling of the amended program projects a weighted-average allowance price near $68 against a $44 baseline. California, Québec and Washington signed a linkage agreement on June 25 for joint auctions, expected to start between late 2027 and early 2028. For tenants: allowance prices feed electricity and diesel costs, and a NNN utility clause that does not distinguish a regulatory surcharge from a rate increase passes the whole move through. A doubling from here is the regulator’s own central case, not a bear case. Check the clause before modeling a 10-year occupancy pro forma.

Perris Measure C warehouse tax

Municipal · Perris operators over 50,000 SF
Watch
Perris voters approved a general business license tax of up to $0.10/SF a year on non-e-commerce warehouse, industrial and manufacturing operators over 50,000 SF with large truck activity, rising up to 3% a year. The county’s semi-final report stamped November 4 put it at 79.50%; the county’s unofficial update of November 5 has it at 80.33%. Neither is certified. The city describes the tax as paid by operators and not as a property tax, and it has not published the ordinance, the start date or the first bill date. For tenants: on the city’s description the bill lands on the occupier, outside the Real Property Taxes paragraph you negotiated, so a tight tax clause does nothing about it. Whether you owe turns on whether the building is principally used to ship online orders to end consumers, a question a customer shift, a subtenant or an assignee can move while the lease stays the same. Issue 10 costs it at $30,000 a year on 300,000 SF and gives the carve-out language.

Local moratoria and mitigation fees

Municipal · Entitlement risk
Watch
Redlands is furthest along. Its Planning Commission recommended Ordinance Text Amendment No. 372 on May 12 by 5–1, and on October 6 the City Council introduced Ordinance No. 3002 on a 5–0 vote, prohibiting new warehouses of 50,000 SF or more, or with more than five truck docks, in every zoning district. The council struck the provision that would have let existing warehouses add truck docks. An existing building may still request height up to its zoning maximum, generally 50 feet, by conditional use permit and with an energy audit showing an efficiency gain; the earlier 15% height cap and the economic-feasibility study requirement are gone. The ordinance returns for final adoption at a later meeting, with no date set. Beaumont, Moreno Valley and Fontana have each rejected recent industrial proposals, and Moreno Valley is negotiating an air-quality abatement fee with the Attorney General’s Office. Moreno Valley also has two drafts in play: PEN26-0058, which strips through-truck status from segments of nine streets, and PEN26-0055, which would require an approved Truck Routing Plan before the city issues a business license to any new tenant of a logistics facility. For tenants: in Redlands the dock-addition path is now closed outright rather than discretionary, so an expansion that depends on new docks in that city fails on the current draft regardless of what the lease says. Elsewhere, an expansion option stops being a right and becomes a discretionary approval with environmental review attached. If your growth plan depends on adding docks or cube to the building you are in, confirm the city will still permit it before you commit to the term.

Macro backdrop

Q2 2026 actuals and current bank forecasts
  • The Fed held at 3.75%–4.00% on October 28, a 10–2 vote with Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack both preferring another quarter point. Inflation “remains elevated” in the statement’s own words; job gains are described as keeping pace with the workforce and activity as expanding at a strong pace. Two dissents for a hike and none for a cut puts the risk on the next move being up, not down.
  • The Fed raised rates on September 16, moving the target range to 3.75%–4.00% on a unanimous 12–0 vote. The statement says inflation “remains elevated” and frames the increase as support for “a timelier return” to the 2% goal.
  • Wells Fargo called it. It moved in August to forecast a hike before year-end, citing tariffs, elevated oil and AI data-center power demand. Bank of America had core PCE easing to 2.8% by Q4, a path that implied cuts. September settled the argument in Wells Fargo’s favor, and the day after the hike Wells Fargo put the funds rate at 4.00%–4.25% by year-end with no changes through 2027, on the reasoning that the excess inflation is supply-driven and the labor market is not overheating. After the October hold that year-end call needs a December move to land.
  • GDP grew 1.5% in Q2, below the 1.8% expected and down from 2.1% in Q1. Consumer spending carried it at a 3.2% annual clip; business investment ex-housing rose 8.4% on AI buildout.
  • Inflation is why. Headline PCE +3.7% year over year, core +3.3%, both well above target, with energy disruption from the Iran conflict feeding through.
  • Labor is tighter than the growth rate suggests. Private payrolls averaged 88,000 a month in the first half, roughly three times last year’s pace; initial claims hit 187,000 in July, the lowest since 1969. Locally the picture is softer: Inland Empire unemployment is 5.5% and the labor force shrank 1.0% over twelve months.
  • CoStar’s September 24 read on the Inland Empire puts vacancy at 8.7% for 2026 Q3 and availability at 12.5%, a 15-year high, against a 6.2% historical average vacancy. Trailing 12-month net absorption is 6.0M SF versus a prior 10-year average of 17.5M SF, deliveries 7.7M SF, and market asking rent growth −0.9%. Construction underway is 14.0M SF, down from the 45.7M SF peak in 2022, with less than 10M SF forecast for completion across 2026 and 2027.
  • CoStar’s October 30 national forecast holds industrial vacancy at its Q3 2026 level of 7.3% through 2027, easing only in 2028, a modest improvement on its prior view. Demand outruns new supply through the rest of 2026, then 2027 net absorption moderates to about 204M SF against roughly 243M SF of completions. CoStar warns that sustained speculative building would prolong elevated vacancy, and names mid-sized logistics product as the exposure. Not every house reads it that way: JLL’s Q3 figures have national vacancy falling rather than flat. The disagreement is directional, not a rounding difference, and the supply pipeline is what separates them.
What a hike does to the renewal question

Relocation is capital. Racking, conveyor, dock equipment, moving the operation, the months of duplicate rent: most of it gets financed, and financing just got more expensive rather than less. That strengthens the case for negotiating hard where you already sit and weakens the cheap-capital case for moving. For owners it cuts the other way on a 2027 refinance, which is the same pressure that makes a landlord willing to trade on rent to keep a covenant in place.

Why it matters here

Loaded imports, not total TEUs, are what convert into pallet positions in Fontana and Perris. Empties are roughly 37% of the combined count, and repositioning them tells you nothing about occupancy demand.
